The City That
Burned

How a kitchen fire destroyed the heart of Thessaloniki, and how the city rebuilt on the ashes was designed to be a different place.

Introduction

A spark on Olympiados Street

On the afternoon of Saturday 18 August 1917, a kitchen fire in a refugee house at 3 Olympiados Street, below the Upper Town, spread to straw stored next door (Lagoudi 2024; National Library of Israel n.d.).1 Driven by a strong wind, it burned for thirty-two hours, destroying about one square kilometre of the historic centre and some 9,500 houses, and leaving more than 70,000 people homeless (Hoad 2017).

This site follows the fire place by place and asks two questions: why did one spark become a catastrophe, and why does the rebuilt city look so unlike the one that burned?

ArgumentThe fire became a turning point that the young Greek state used to remake a multi-faith Ottoman port as a planned, modern and Hellenic city (Mazower 2004; Yerolympos 1996).
The story in eight chapters
    Chapter 02 · 1912–1917

    A crowded city at war

    In 1917 Thessaloniki had been Greek for only five years. Until 1912 it was an Ottoman port where Sephardic Jews formed the largest community, living alongside Muslims and Orthodox Christians in neighbourhoods rarely exclusive to any one group (Mazower 2004; University of Michigan Library n.d.). Its centre was a dense web of winding lanes, timber-framed houses, bazaars, synagogues, mosques and churches, grown over centuries without an overall plan (Yerolympos 1996).

    The First World War made the city more crowded and more fragile. From 1915 it was the base of the Allied armies on the Macedonian front, with up to 200,000 soldiers in and around it. The Allies controlled the water reserves for their camps, a summer drought had already reduced supply, and the city had no public fire brigade, only private teams paid by insurance companies (Papastathis 1978). Timber, wind, drought and war: the conditions for disaster were already in place.

    Chapter 03 · 18–19 August 1917

    Thirty-two hours

    Step through the fire hour by hour. The image changes as the fire moves through the city.

    0hours

    1. The fire starts in the Mevlane quarter. With little water to hand, it is not put out in time (Lagoudi 2024).

    2. Driven by the Vardaris wind,2 one front runs along Agiou Dimitriou, the other down Leontos Sofou towards the market (Papastathis 1978).

    3. Allied soldiers blow up buildings to stop the flames, but in places the explosions only spread them (Papastathis 1978).

    4. The two fronts merge over the commercial centre between Egnatia Street and the sea, which burns almost completely (Papastathis 1978).

    5. The flames pass around the Byzantine church and push east to Hamidie Street, today Ethnikis Amynis.3

    6. After thirty-two hours the fire dies down. The “burned zone” covers about 32 per cent of the city (Papastathis 1978).

      The fire destroyed civic and religious landmarks along with homes: the Town Hall, the Post Office, the water and gas companies, the Saatli Mosque and much of the basilica of Agios Dimitrios (Lagoudi 2024). With the shops and workshops gone, most of the city's workers lost their livelihoods overnight (Papastathis 1978).

      The losses were not shared equally. The official Pallis Report counted about 52,000 Jewish, 11,000 Muslim and 10,000 Orthodox Christian homeless (Papastathis 1978). Synagogues, schools and the Chief Rabbinate's centuries-old archive disappeared, so the Jewish community had to rebuild even its register of members (National Library of Israel n.d.). Greek and Allied authorities set up tent camps and bread distribution, but relief met only immediate needs (Papastathis 1978).

      Chapter 06 · 1917–1920s

      A new city on the ashes

      After earlier fires, owners had rebuilt on their old plots. This time Eleftherios Venizelos's government decided that the old centre would not return (Mazower 2004). Minister Alexandros Papanastasiou set up an international committee led by the French architect and archaeologist Ernest Hébrard, already in the city with the French army's archaeological mission, together with Thomas Mawson, Aristotelis Zachos and Konstantinos Kitsikis (Lagoudi 2024; Yerolympos 1996).

      Their 1918 plan replaced the lanes with wide avenues, regular blocks, squares and parks that let in light and air (Lagoudi 2024). Its centrepiece was a monumental axis rising from the sea through a new square, today's Aristotelous Square, towards a planned civic centre, framed by façades drawing on Byzantine and Western rather than Ottoman models (Yerolympos 1996). Many grander ideas were never built for lack of money, but the modern centre's street pattern is still largely Hébrard's.

      Chapter 07 · Interpretation

      Who was the new city for?

      Historians still debate what the reconstruction meant. For some it was an overdue modernisation of a crowded, unhealthy city; for others it Hellenised Thessaloniki at the expense of its Jewish majority (Rozen 2017). Many Salonican Jews saw the law that reparcelled the burned zone, instead of returning old plots, as a scheme to push them out of the centre, and many families later emigrated (Rozen 2017; National Library of Israel n.d.).

      Yet Rozen also shows that some of the Jewish elite adapted to the Greek state and profited from the rebuilding, so the story is not simply one of planners and victims (Rozen 2017).

      Reading the sources critically

      The sources do not always agree. Open each question to see where they differ.

      • Careful accounts give about 1 km², roughly 32 per cent of the city (Papastathis 1978; Hoad 2017). Popular articles often say “two thirds”, probably confusing the city with its historic centre.

      • The 1917 memorandum lists 16 of 33 destroyed (Papastathis 1978); Jewish community sources say 32 (National Library of Israel n.d.), perhaps counting small prayer houses.

      • Totals agree at about 70,000, but the breakdown by community differs between sources (University of Michigan Library n.d.). Compare them in the chart in Chapter 05.

      • One heritage source dates it to the late 15th century (Google Arts & Culture n.d.), the Greek tourism organisation to after 1530 (Visit Greece n.d.).

      Chapter 08 · Conclusion

      Walking the axis today

      Walking up from the sea through Aristotelous Square today, it is easy to forget that this orderly view was born from catastrophe. In thirty-two hours the fire destroyed the fabric of Ottoman Salonica; the plan that followed ensured it would not return. To understand the fire is to see both at once: a modern city of light and air, and the mixed neighbourhoods whose streets lie beneath it (Mazower 2004; Naar 2016).

      Chapter 09 · About this project

      Concept, process & sources

      Concept

      A journey through space and time: the chapters follow the fire in order, from the city before 1917 to the one built after it, and the map ties each chapter to places that can still be visited.

      Locations

      I chose eight places that mark the fire's start, path and limits, and the axis of the new plan, so the reader sees the old city and the new one side by side.

      Creative process

      I began with historical studies and Greece's National Documentation Centre archive, drew the burned zone from official boundary streets, then paired each chapter with archive images and my own photographs. Ember colours mark the fire; pale tones mark the rebuilding.

      Notes

      1. Greece still used the Julian calendar in 1917, so local documents date the fire to 5 August (Old Style). The court investigation concluded that it was an accident (Papastathis 1978).
      2. The Vardaris is the strong northerly wind that blows down the Axios (Vardar) valley towards the Thermaic Gulf. Greek accounts sometimes remember the fire as the “Fiery Vardaris” (Lagoudi 2024).
      3. The official boundary streets of the burned zone were Agiou Dimitriou, Leontos Sofou, Nikis, Ethnikis Amynis (formerly Hamidie), Alexandrou Svolou and Egnatia (Papastathis 1978; Lagoudi 2024).
